# Design the app around missed weeks

The app that survives is the one that asks almost nothing of its owner on a bad day and still gives something back, so the design should be organised around lapses, load and trust and not around feature count. The research supports a specific shape: a Telegram thread that speaks in at most three scheduled batches a day, a desktop planner that shows planned hours against real capacity and proposes every change as a reviewable diff, and a phone surface built on a "status dial plus timeline" that is nearly identical to its own widgets. Four of the owner's earlier decisions should change: Google Calendar OAuth is reachable without the paid security assessment, Anthropic's linked billing (announced the day the research was done) should replace stored API keys where it is available, message batching should sit on top of the 5/10 cap, and gym deloads should be reactive and not scheduled. Telegram survives the all-Apple re-test because no sanctioned iMessage route is open to an individual. The recommended stack is Postgres with row-level security, an offline-capable sync layer, a durable job runner and the Claude Messages API behind a quarantined ingestion pipeline with a staging inbox. The evidence is uneven: if-then planning, progress monitoring, notification batching and slack-based targets have experimental support, while the numeric defaults (the 5/10 cap, the 1.5 estimate multiplier, the project caps) are judgement calls and are labelled as such below. Several technical points the stack rests on were not verified by the researchers and are listed as spikes at the end.

## Decisions for you

These are the choices where evidence cannot settle the matter because the answer depends on what you want the product to feel like. Each has a recommendation.

**2. Is the phone "a window you open" or "present on the Lock Screen"?** Either a Home Screen web app only (no Apple fee, review or rules; no widgets, share-sheet target, background refresh or HealthKit), or a native app distributed through TestFlight and later as an unlisted App Store app (widgets, Live Activities, share extension, HealthKit; $99 a year, Apple review, and builds that expire after 90 days). *Recommendation: present on the Lock Screen (native), with the web app shipped first as the permanent rule-free fallback.* Reason: the three native features that matter here (passive sleep and workout data, share-to-save, a glanceable widget) each remove a daily effort, and effort and forgetting are the leading documented reasons people stop logging.

**3. Must relatives install Telegram, or do you add an iMessage relay line?** Either "sanctioned and free, one install" (Telegram plus the in-app thread only), or "zero install for relatives, paid and against Apple's rules" (add a relay line beside them). *Recommendation: Telegram only at launch; add a relay later only if a specific relative refuses the install.* Reason: relays cost roughly $100 to $250 per line per month, can be banned without appeal, and a reminder bot's outbound-heavy pattern is the reported ban trigger.

**4. Default position on the bluntness dial.** Pick one of four levels, all of which still schedule the task: Note (a one-line load note in the daily summary), Flag (says in the reply what is now at risk), Challenge (asks one question and proposes a specific trade, then schedules), Argue (recommends against using your own history, requires "do it anyway", records the override). *Recommendation: Flag as the default for invitees, Challenge for you.* Reason: default assistants over-affirm and that measurably harms users, but no study supports harsh settings and the one product with an abrasive default was badly received.

**5. Default autonomy preset.** Pick one of Cautious (asks before anything beyond ingestion and held drafts), Balanced (makes reversible in-app edits and tells you; researches and drafts quietly), Hands-off (also edits app-owned calendar blocks quietly). *Recommendation: Cautious for invitees, Balanced for you.* Reason: every public agent failure the researchers found involved sending, deleting or acting for the user unasked; anything addressed to another person, spending money or deleting stays "ask first" under every preset.

**6. A guard against rebuilding the system.** Either structural settings (areas, tags, views, persona) are changeable any time, or they are locked outside a recurring "system change" window and mid-window ideas go to a list. *Recommendation: accept the lock.* Reason: it guards against rebuilding the system instead of using it; it is the researcher's own inference with no study behind it, which is why it is yours to decide.

**7. Accountability sharing.** Either the app is strictly private per user, or each user can opt in to send a weekly summary of completed progress to one chosen person. *Recommendation: offer the opt-in, off by default.* Reason: progress monitoring works better when progress is reported to someone, whereas publicly announcing intentions can reduce follow-through.

## Calm delivery beats clever features in every strand of evidence

### Three batches a day, not a number of messages

The earlier design capped proactive messages at five (soft) and ten (hard) per day. Nothing found contradicts those numbers and nothing supports them; no study of daily caps for assistants turned up, and the industry article usually quoted for notification thresholds is an informal poll of 14 staff ([Braze](https://www.braze.com/resources/articles/opt-out-of-push-notifications-why-users-do-it)). What the evidence does address is the delivery pattern. A two-week field experiment with over 200 participants found that people who got their notifications in **three fixed batches a day** reported better mood, attention and felt productivity, while hourly batches did little and a no-notifications arm felt more anxious ([authors' summary](https://communities.springernature.com/posts/batching-notifications-can-improve-attention-well-being-and-productivity)). That study covered Indian smartphone users and all notifications, so applying it to one app's messages is an inference. The decision is therefore to keep the caps as a ceiling and add three user-timed slots: a morning message carrying the plan, the digest and the check-in buttons; a mid-afternoon slot that stays silent when empty; and an evening close. Only two kinds of message break out of a batch, a start cue the user set and a calendar change that is imminent, with a default limit of two a day. That limit, and the rule that a message type ignored three times running halves its frequency, are judgement calls.

Timing matters as much as volume. In a controlled study of a proactive coding assistant, **53.3 percent of interventions were engaged with, 34.7 percent ignored and 12.1 percent disruptive**, and the best moments were subtask boundaries, not idle periods ([arXiv 2502.18658](https://arxiv.org/html/2502.18658v4)). The sample was 18 programmers, so treat the figures as a rough baseline. OpenAI drew the same conclusion from retiring ChatGPT Pulse in June 2026, saying proactive features need to be personalised, action-oriented and steerable ([Digit](https://www.digit.in/news/general/openai-is-retiring-chatgpt-pulse-and-replacing-it-with-scheduled-tasks-here-is-why.html)). The digest is the module most exposed to Pulse's fate, so it ships as a visible scheduled task with sections the user can switch off, retime and pause, and every proactive message traces to a rule the user can see and disable.

### Slack, repair and a soft landing after silence

Consumer retention is poor everywhere: vendor benchmarks put productivity apps near 4 percent of installers at day 30 ([Sendbird roundup](https://sendbird.com/blog/app-retention-benchmarks-broken-down-by-industry), a blog aggregating vendor reports with differing definitions), and a peer-reviewed panel study found a 3.3 percent median for mental-health apps ([Baumel et al.](https://www.jmir.org/2019/9/e14567)). A motivated invite-only group will not track mass-market installs. The reasons people give for stopping are consistent: among lapsed food journalers, fading value led at 37 percent and effort followed at 25 percent ([Cordeiro et al.](https://homes.cs.washington.edu/~jfogarty/publications/chi2015-decaf.pdf)), and about half of lapsed Fitbit users surveyed felt guilt or frustration and mostly wanted to come back ([Epstein et al.](https://homes.cs.washington.edu/~jfogarty/publications/ubicomp2016.pdf)).

Streak design decides what happens the day after a miss. Seven studies showed engagement rising while a streak is displayed intact and falling once it is shown broken, with the fall worse under self-blame and milder when repair is allowed ([CU Boulder summary](https://www.colorado.edu/business/faculty-research/2023/04/19/or-track-how-broken-streaks-affect-consumer-decisions)). In a month-long step challenge, goals with an "emergency reserve" of two skips a week produced a **55 percent rebound the day after a miss against 37 percent for a strict goal** ([UCLA Anderson Review](https://anderson-review.ucla.edu/emergency-reserves/)). One missed day does not measurably damage habit formation ([UCL News](https://www.ucl.ac.uk/news/2009/aug/how-long-does-it-take-form-habit)). The app therefore shows rates that one miss cannot break, gives every recurring target a labelled reserve, and makes the first message after a miss the smallest version of today's behaviour, never a report of the gap.

Re-entry is designed as a feature. After several days of silence the system pauses itself: overdue flags hide, recurring items stop accumulating, and the single next contact offers a short reset. After a longer absence it offers backlog bankruptcy, which moves everything undated or overdue to a parked list and invites the user to bring back three items. Views tolerate missing data, with no zero-filled days and no cumulative overdue count anywhere. A user-set status (sick, travelling, on a break) silences expectations in every module, a pattern taken from Gentler Streak ([Sketch](https://sketch.com/blog/gentler-streak)). These re-entry rules are practitioner advice with indirect support; the researchers found no peer-reviewed work on "system hopping", and the fresh-start effect that would favour Monday re-entry offers was recalled but not verified.

### Planning defaults that have experiments behind them

If-then planning is among the best-supported techniques in the field, with a pooled effect of d = 0.65 across 94 tests in the 2006 meta-analysis ([Konstanz repository](https://kops.uni-konstanz.de/entities/publication/2e749bfb-8533-437c-8203-7e788c910c5f)); a 2024 update covering 642 tests is known here only through a secondary summary, and the 0.65 figure is an upper-range result uncorrected for publication bias. The limit is decisive for this product: when participants planned six goals instead of one, the benefit vanished ([EurekAlert](https://www.eurekalert.org/news-releases/749027)). So only the day's one to three must-do items get a start cue and a first physical action ("after my 2pm lecture ends, I open the doc and write the first paragraph"), confirmed once by the user in their own words. Only those items are blocked on the calendar. No randomised trial compares time blocking with a plain list, so full-day blocking is not justified, and the rule of leaving roughly 40 percent of free hours unscheduled is practitioner convention.

Time estimates need recorded history, not memory. The classic thesis study, known here through Wikipedia's summary, had students predicting 33.9 days and taking 55.5 ([Planning fallacy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Planning_fallacy)). Across three experiments, forecasts improved once people were shown measured durations, which recall alone does not achieve ([Roy et al.](https://jayscholar.etown.edu/facpubharvest/525)); backward planning from a deadline also removed most of the optimistic bias in four studies ([Wiebe, Buehler et al.](https://dlab.sauder.ubc.ca/sjdm/journal/16/16101/jdm16101.html)). The app therefore shows the user's own ratio at the moment of estimating ("your last five writing tasks ran 1.8 times the estimate"), applies it per category when placing blocks, breaks down only tasks that are large or multi-step, and plans deadline work backward on desktop. The cold-start multiplier of 1.5 is the researcher's choice, loosely anchored on the thesis study, and no trial has tested months of estimate feedback inside a task app.

The same arithmetic powers pushback. Calendar blocks after the multiplier, summed against available hours, give a deterministic overcommitment signal; the model only chooses the words. Load is shown in hours and not as a count of projects, because the evidence concerns competition for time: accuracy fell steadily with task switching in a lab experiment ([Adler and Benbunan-Fich](https://www.interruptions.net/literature/Adler-IntJHumComputStud12.pdf)) and a narrative review links goal conflict to worse performance and wellbeing ([Gray et al.](https://www.frontiersin.org/articles/10.3389/fpsyg.2017.02011)). No study supports any specific cap, so the soft limit of about three active projects per area is a judgement that triggers a "which one gets parked?" question and never a block.

Procrastination-specific evidence is thin. The one intervention meta-analysis pooled 12 trials and 718 participants for g = 0.34, with risk of bias throughout ([Rozental et al.](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/psychology/articles/10.3389/fpsyg.2018.01588/full)). The app's levers are therefore the adjacent, better-supported ones. Steel's meta-analysis identifies task aversiveness and distance from the deadline as consistent predictors ([Calgary repository](https://prism.ucalgary.ca/items/d0dea740-4751-4e9a-84da-2d8ad446830d/full)), which justifies near-term checkpoints for anything due far out and a five-minute starter action for tasks that sit untouched. The well-known self-imposed-deadlines experiment is not part of that justification; its data have been reported as anomalous and it failed to replicate ([Data Colada](https://datacolada.org/138)). The product's distinctive lever is the agent doing the aversive first step itself and handing over a started artefact. Body doubling has no effectiveness evidence and stays an optional experiment.

Reflection earns its place but not as a long ritual. Across 138 randomised experiments, interventions that got people to monitor progress lifted goal attainment by d = 0.40, more so when progress was physically recorded or reported to someone ([Harkin et al.](https://eprints.whiterose.ac.uk/id/eprint/91437/)), and a field experiment found that 15 minutes of daily written reflection raised trainees' final test scores by about 23 percent ([HBS Working Knowledge](https://www.library.hbs.edu/working-knowledge/reflecting-on-work-improves-job-performance)). No trial isolates the weekly cadence. The weekly review is therefore short, agent-prepared, opens with what got done, asks three questions and never blocks anything when skipped; a two-line evening close carries the daily reflection. A debrief meta-analysis that would strengthen the action-report idea was recalled by the researcher but not verified, so it is not relied on here.

## One agent that argues from your own numbers

### Copy Sunsama's review screen and Linear's triage

The closest shipped precedent for the chief of staff is Sunsama's 2026 planning agent. The user types intent, the agent returns a plan as a list of changes grouped by day, uncertain choices appear as "judgement calls" with three options each, every action has undo, and a short briefing states what the agent declined to do and why ([Sunsama help](https://help.sunsama.com/docs/usage-guides/daily-planning)). Its "You're out of time today" prompt offers to extend the day or move the work and then decides by default, which is pushback without refusal. The desktop should reproduce that review screen nearly wholesale, and pair it with a hidden-by-default intake queue that uses Linear's four single-key verbs of accept, decline, merge and snooze ([Linear docs](https://linear.app/docs/triage)). Horizon planning follows the same source: three to five weekly objectives, tasks attached to them, a "does it fit" check, and a review showing time spent per objective ([Sunsama help](https://help.sunsama.com/docs/weekly-planning)). Much of the comparative material on desktop planners came from competitor-published posts, and common-knowledge patterns such as the command palette were not sourced in this round.

Auto-scheduling is where this class of product loses trust. Reviews of Reclaim describe fixed meetings being moved and calendars packed until they feel overwhelming ([Hackceleration](https://hackceleration.com/labs/review/reclaim)), a picture that rests on one review's summary of user reports. For anyone prone to overcommitment, a scheduler that always finds room hides the problem. The rules are fixed accordingly: items with an explicit time never move, every block has a lock, rescheduling arrives as a diff to accept, slack is left by default, and the app's schedule lives in its own calendar feed so no ghost events land in the user's real calendar.

### A behavioural dial, a matrix of permissions

Pushback is justified by a March 2026 study in Science, reported by AP: across 11 AI systems chatbots affirmed users' actions **49 percent more often than humans did**, people exposed to over-affirming AI grew more certain and less willing to change course, and they preferred it anyway ([AP via KSAT](https://www.ksat.com/business/2026/03/26/ai-is-giving-bad-advice-to-flatter-its-users-says-new-study-on-dangers-of-overly-agreeable-chatbots/)). No controlled study of "tough love" settings exists, so the four-level dial is reasoned design and not evidence. What stays constant across levels is the important part: the evidence is always the user's own numbers, there is no sarcasm, there is one round of argument at most, an override is recorded and the same objection is not repeated. The dial softens one level automatically when the check-in shows poor sleep or low mood, and the weekly review shows whether past objections turned out right. Expect users to pick low settings, since people prefer affirmation.

Autonomy is a matrix of action classes against three modes (ask first; do it, then tell me; do it quietly and log it) and not a single level, following the published five-level framework that treats autonomy as a design choice separate from capability ([arXiv 2506.12469](https://arxiv.org/html/2506.12469v2)). Ingestion is always on and silent, as you decided. Reversible in-app edits default to "do it, then tell me". Research and drafting run quietly with output held as a draft. Changes outside the app ask first. Anything addressed to another person, spending money or deleting asks first under every preset, with per-recipe opt-in only. This constrains your "agent starts the steps it can do itself" decision to drafting and preparation in the first version. The grounds are concrete: Lindy added a mandatory approver for write actions following reports of emails reaching the wrong recipients ([Saner.AI](https://blog.saner.ai/lindy-ai-reviews/), a competitor blog), and OpenClaw users saw an agent delete an inbox and send an unrequested letter to an insurer ([TechRadar Pro](https://www.techradar.com/pro/here-are-the-openclaw-security-risks-you-should-know-about)). The preset contents and the idea of offering to upgrade a class after a run of unedited approvals are proposals with no published evaluation.

### Memory in files, lessons with triggers, and a staging area for everything inbound

Anthropic's November 2026 engineering post describes four memory layers, from searchable transcripts at the bottom to a small injected context at the top, with an offline pass that checks notes against transcripts, merges and prunes ([Anthropic](https://anthropic.com/engineering/components-of-agent-memory)). It also reports the failure modes: notes wrong from the start, drift, bloat, and an incorrect top-layer note contaminating every session. Letta, a vendor, reports that an agent using plain file tools scored 74.0 percent on a memory benchmark against 68.5 percent for a graph-based system ([Letta](https://www.letta.com/blog/benchmarking-ai-agent-memory)). The design here follows: a small profile loaded on every run, topical notes per module, the database as the source of truth the agent queries, raw transcripts kept searchable, and nightly consolidation on a cheap model. Claude's memory tool is client-side, so the app's handler decrypts and encrypts per user and the tool fits the envelope-encryption design ([memory tool docs](https://platform.claude.com/docs/en/agents-and-tools/tool-use/memory-tool)).

Always-on background ingestion creates a specific risk. A 2026 paper, read only through a third-party summary whose own figures vary, found that plausible false content an agent merely read during background runs was often promoted into long-term memory when those runs shared a session with user chat ([summary of arXiv 2603.23064](https://ai-paper-delta.vercel.app/ko/papers/2603.23064)). The mitigations are structural: background runs are isolated from the chat session, every memory entry carries provenance and a date, and nothing inferred or externally sourced reaches the always-loaded profile without the user's confirmation.

Action reports feed planning in the form the literature supports. A 2026 workshop paper found that distilling each finished task into a heuristic with a trigger condition lifted benchmark success from 48.3 to 56.1 percent, while injecting raw past trajectories scored below the baseline ([alphaXiv 2603.24639](https://www.alphaxiv.org/abs/2603.24639)); Anthropic separately found that recording only failures reduced the benefit of memory. So the report stores the user's raw words, the measured estimate and actual, and an agent-written lesson as trigger plus instruction. Three paths use it: estimate calibration in code, category-level lessons merged nightly with a count of supporting reports (one report means "unconfirmed"), and a handful of lessons retrieved once at breakdown time. The agent shows the lesson in use so the user sees a return on the report.

The ingestion pipeline is the security boundary. Prompt injection through email is unsolved at the model level; the "lethal trifecta" analysis holds that an agent with private data, untrusted content and an outbound channel is exploitable, and that removing any one leg defeats the attack ([Tian Pan](https://tianpan.co/blog/2026/01/25/the-lethal-trifecta-ai-agents)). Every inbound item therefore lands in a durable queue with the raw payload kept encrypted, passes deterministic pre-processing, and goes to a quarantined extraction call with a fixed schema, no tools and no memory. Constrained decoding guarantees the output parses, though numeric ranges still need validating in code and a refusal can still break the schema ([structured outputs docs](https://platform.claude.com/docs/en/build-with-claude/structured-outputs)). Code then validates dates, durations and duplicates and routes by consequence: low-stakes records write silently; medium ones write and appear in the next batch; anything with an uncertain date, amount or person, or anything that would create a calendar block, is held in the staging inbox for the user. Instructions found inside an email become a field at most, never a command. The desktop inbox shows what each raw item became with one-tap correction. Model self-reported confidence is a hint only, and thresholds will need tuning on the group's own data.

On runtime the two researchers disagreed. One recommends the plain Messages API with a hand-written tool loop throughout, since the Agent SDK is Claude Code packaged as a library with file and shell tools ([Agent SDK overview](https://code.claude.com/docs/en/agent-sdk/overview)); the other sees its permissions and hooks as a natural fit for the autonomy matrix but did not verify that it runs sensibly on a multi-tenant server with a different key per session. The decision is the Messages API with your own loop, with the SDK question settled by a spike. Models route by job: the cheapest for extraction and consolidation, a mid-tier model for planning and chat, a larger one for weekly review and long-horizon planning, user-selectable because the user pays. One ingestion run on the cheapest current model was estimated at well under a tenth of a cent, but the price table was extracted incompletely and the two researchers recorded different figures for the top model, so confirm prices before showing users a spend meter ([Claude pricing](https://platform.claude.com/docs/en/about-claude/pricing)). That meter, by day and job type, is itself a requirement: opaque consumption is a top complaint about Lindy.

## Three surfaces, each on the least fragile route

### Telegram survives the all-Apple re-test

No public iMessage API exists. Apple Messages for Business is open only to official brand owners working through an authorised provider and several Apple reviews ([Apple Business Register](https://register.apple.com/resources/messages/messaging-documentation/register-your-acct)); the one AI agent approved so far, Poke, is a venture-funded company that pays Apple per user ([AppleInsider](https://appleinsider.com/articles/26/06/04/first-ai-agent-for-messages-business-chat-approved-by-apple)). Everything else relays through real Apple IDs on vendor hardware. Lindy's engineering write-up records Apple permanently banning its account on launch day, with no published limits and no appeal, and names a lopsided send-to-receive ratio as a contributing factor ([Lindy](https://www.lindy.ai/blog/imessage-api-three-rewrites-one-apple-ban-and-what-actually-works)). A quiet user who receives five reminders a day and replies weekly is precisely that profile. Relay pricing rests on weak sources: about $100 per line per month for Sendblue according to a third-party blog that also describes the plan as inbound-first ([Kanal](https://getkanal.com/blog/sendblue-pricing)), and about $250 plus setup for Linq according to its competitor ([Sendblue](https://www.sendblue.com/compare/sendblue-vs-linq)). With a relay, the vendor can also read every message.

SMS is legal for an individual through Twilio's sole-proprietor registration but needs a public website, policies and manual vetting, offers no buttons, and is readable by carriers ([Twilio](https://www.twilio.com/docs/messaging/compliance/a2p-10dlc/direct-sole-proprietor-registration-overview-new%20experience)). WhatsApp is out because Meta bars general-purpose AI assistants from its Business Platform, a point sourced from a vendor summary of Meta's terms ([respond.io](https://respond.io/blog/whatsapp-chatbot-policy-2026)). Telegram bots message users free ([Bots FAQ](https://core.telegram.org/bots/faq)), and the 2025 to 2026 Bot API releases added streaming drafts, topics inside private chats, coloured buttons, ephemeral messages and, on 9 November 2026, HTML widgets ([changelog](https://core.telegram.org/bots/api-changelog)). Private-chat topics allow separate Capture, Reminders and Digest lanes in one chat. Two caveats stand: the statement that bot chats are not end-to-end encrypted is the researcher's prior knowledge and uncited, and no data exists on how willingly relatives install a second messenger. The channel sits behind one queue so caps apply per user and not per channel, every reminder is answerable by a short typed reply as well as a button, and onboarding is always user-initiated. Poke and Lindy both run several channels behind one agent, which is the pattern to copy. If Apple opens Messages for Business to small agents, that would overtake every option here and should be re-checked before launch.

### The phone starts as a web app and is designed widget-first

A Home Screen web app on iOS 26 gets full-screen launch, web push, badges and generous storage with no Apple account or review; it does not get background sync, a share-sheet target, widgets or an install prompt, and push works only after the user adds it by hand ([firt.dev](https://firt.dev/notes/pwa-ios/)). Since iOS 26, any site added to the Home Screen opens as a web app by default. Because the server holds the source of truth, storage eviction is a nuisance and not a data-loss risk, provided unsynced data never lives only on the phone. The real failure modes are human: the manual install, the notification permission tap that must happen inside the installed app, and links opened in an in-app browser. Whether iOS web push supports action buttons and how reliably it delivers were not sourced, and web push only reaches people who finished the install, which is another reason the chat thread remains the dependable reminder channel.

Native adds what the web cannot. TestFlight allows up to 10,000 external testers but each build expires after 90 days, and the first external build gets a full review ([Apple](https://developer.apple.com/help/app-store-connect/test-a-beta-version/testflight-overview)). Unlisted App Store distribution is permanent and link-only but needs full review plus a separate request Apple can decline, and a friends-and-family app is not among Apple's example audiences ([Apple](https://developer.apple.com/support/unlisted-app-distribution)). The review rules that bite are specific: personal data shared with third-party AI needs explicit prior consent naming the provider under guideline 5.1.2(i), reviewers need a working demo account, and in-app account deletion is mandatory ([App Review Guidelines](https://developer.apple.com/app-store/review/guidelines/)). Nothing in the guidelines addresses user-supplied keys, and no documented approval or rejection over them was found. Health data carries its own limits, so the readiness score is computed deterministically on the server and the model sees only the derived number. The provider-named consent screen is built in every client regardless.

Apple's widget guidance shapes the visual design whichever route you pick. Small widgets carry one fact, interactivity is limited to buttons and toggles, real-time progress belongs in Live Activities, and Lock Screen rendering is desaturated so meaning cannot depend on colour ([Apple HIG](https://developer.apple.com/design/human-interface-guidelines/widgets)). Designing the small widget first forces the one-fact decision, which is why the Status Dial is recommended as the top strip. The gesture vocabulary has shipped precedent in Things 3: drag the add button to place an item, long-press to move, swipe right to schedule, swipe left to select, tap to expand in place ([MacStories](https://www.macstories.net/reviews/things-3-beauty-and-delight-in-a-task-manager/)). Apple Shortcuts can post text, JSON or a file to a per-user endpoint ([Apple](https://support.apple.com/guide/shortcuts/apd58d46713f/ios)), covering dictated capture and share-sheet saves without native code; the share-sheet, import-question and Health-sample capabilities are stated from general knowledge and need testing on a device.

### Modules ordered by dependency and by harm risk

The gym design you settled matches what the best-regarded products ship. MacroFactor states that its progression is rule-based and not generative AI ([MacroFactor](https://macrofactor.com/workouts/)), and JuggernautAI adjusts each session from pre-session questions on sleep, mood, energy and soreness ([JuggernautAI help](https://help.jtsstrength.com/en/articles/3-how-juggernautai-is-individualized-to-you)). The changes come from the literature. A randomised trial of 39 trained lifters found that a week off mid-programme produced no size advantage and **less strength gain than training straight through**, and its authors caution against prescribing a deload to someone who does not feel the need ([Coleman et al.](https://peerj.com/articles/16777)); the deload tested was full cessation, so reduced volume triggered by stalled estimated one-rep max and several low-readiness days is the reasoned alternative. Autoregulated loading showed a small, non-significant strength advantage over fixed loading in a meta-analysis that also found effort ratings often inaccurate ([Hickmott et al.](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1186/s40798-021-00404-9)), so readiness is a bounded modifier weighted lightly until calibrated. Wearable composite scores are not a gate: the one study cited, small, in swimmers and known through a secondary commentary, found WHOOP's scores unrelated to measured stress ([CTS](https://trainright.com/new-study-reveals-holes-in-wearable-device-scores/)).

The check-in rides inside the morning message so it costs nothing against the cap. It asks sleep quality on a 0 to 10 scale adapted from a validated single item ([PsyToolkit](https://psytoolkit.org/survey-library/single-item-sleep-quality-scale.html)), mood or energy on five points, and soreness only after a logged workout. Adapting the item to "last night" changes its validated recall window, and the two researchers proposed different scales, so the choice is a judgement. Compliance evidence comes from short youth research studies, where two to three prompts a day held 91.7 percent compliance against about 75 percent for more ([Wen et al.](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC5425774)); nothing measures months of voluntary adult use, so expect decay. No module depends on Apple Health, which only a native app can read ([Momentum](https://www.themomentum.ai/blog/what-you-can-and-cant-do-with-apple-healthkit-data), a vendor blog).

Food is the module with the most accuracy and wellbeing risk. The best general vision models erred by roughly 21 to 25 percent on meal calories from one photo ([Coburn et al.](https://arxiv.org/html/2507.07048v2)), and an NIH conference abstract not yet peer reviewed found four consumer apps undercounting by about 250 to 345 kcal per meal, mostly fat ([ScienceDaily](https://sciencedaily.com/releases/2026/07/260726015237.htm)). Photo-first capture nonetheless cut "too hard to enter" skipping from 60 to 22 percent in a field study ([Cordeiro et al.](https://homes.cs.washington.edu/~jfogarty/publications/chi2015-decaf.pdf)). The module therefore serves pattern review and not calorie targets: the model lists items and grams, nutrients come from USDA FoodData Central, which is public domain ([FDC](https://fdc.nal.usda.gov/api-guide)), and Open Food Facts barcode data stays in a separate table because of its share-alike licence, a licence reading and not legal advice. Numbers are hidden by default with no red-green marking and no comment at capture time. Those precautions follow from correlational evidence in a clinical sample ([Levinson et al.](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC5700836)) and are not proven harm reducers.

Digests start with what is licence-clean. arXiv metadata and abstracts are CC0 and may be stored and transformed, with one request every three seconds across all your machines ([arXiv terms](https://info.arxiv.org/help/api/tou.html)), so one shared nightly fetch serves everyone and ranking happens per user. News comes last: in the EBU and BBC study, 45 percent of assistant answers about news had a significant issue ([The Register](https://theregister.com/2025/10/24/bbc_probe_ai_news)), a figure for assistants answering questions and so an upper-bound warning for grounded summarisation. News items are summarised only from fetched text with the link beside each. Weather, poetry and news-publisher terms were not verified. Saves split into two schedulers: unread items resurface on an expanding schedule to force a decision, and highlights use Readwise-style weighted sampling ([Readwise docs](https://docs.readwise.io/highlights/docs/faqs/reviewing-highlights)). No study supports passive highlight resurfacing as a memory aid, so it is framed as re-encounter.

## A stack chosen for isolation and an honest privacy promise

### Postgres with row-level security, offline sync and a durable job runner

At 5 to 30 users every option's lowest tier covers the data, so the choice is about fragility and isolation. Of the sync engines checked, PowerSync is the one packaged and priced for offline clients over an existing Postgres ([PowerSync](https://www.powersync.com/pricing)); Zero reached 1.0 in 2026 but its hosted cloud is not yet generally available ([Zero status](https://zero.rocicorp.dev/docs/status.mdx.md)); InstantDB's cloud shuts down in August 2027 and is excluded ([Instant](https://instantdb.com/essays/instant_team_joins_openai)). Trigger.dev imposes no maximum run duration, which suits LLM calls that run for minutes ([Trigger.dev](https://trigger.dev/pricing)). The researcher's sum of cited prices puts running costs at roughly **$85 to $125 a month** before a domain, key management and error tracking, excluding users' own model spend; the Convex runner-up would cost far less. The recommendation rests on points the researcher flagged as unverified: that PowerSync has a client for whichever phone route is chosen, that Zero and Convex lack offline writes, and that row-level security is the right isolation barrier. Application-layer encryption also interacts with sync rules, since a sync engine replicating encrypted rows pushes key delivery to the client.

### Linked billing first, stored keys second

Anthropic announced linked billing in beta on 17 November 2026: the user approves the app in an Anthropic-hosted flow, sets a monthly spend limit and can unlink at any time, and the app never holds a key ([Anthropic](https://claude.com/resources/articles/linked-billing-on-claude-platform)). Two consequences need stating. Requests execute inside the platform's organisation, so the user's own organisation settings do not apply and all traffic falls under the owner's retention policy. And no eligibility rules were published, so whether a small unincorporated app can register is unknown. Either route still requires each relative to have an Anthropic account with billing, which is the hard part for non-technical users. Users cannot power the app from a Claude subscription. If keys are stored, each is encrypted with a per-user data key wrapped by a key-management service, decrypted only inside the worker at call time, never logged, and shown by its last four characters; that is standard practice the researcher did not source. Public shared content (arXiv, the food database, weather) has no natural owner under bring-your-own-key, so a small owner-funded system key pays for it, with cost scaling by sources and not by users. Keep the model boundary provider-agnostic even though linked billing is Anthropic-specific.

### Calendar and email without restricted scopes

The earlier rule against Google OAuth was sound for Gmail and too broad for calendars. Google's own documentation shows an app in production but unverified can be authorised by any Google account, subject to a warning screen and a lifetime cap of 100 new users, with the seven-day token expiry described only for testing status ([Google](https://support.google.com/cloud/answer/15549945?authuser=7)). The classification of calendar scopes as sensitive and Gmail read scopes as restricted comes from one secondary guide ([Nango](https://nango.dev/docs/api-integrations/google-shared/google-security-review)), which also contradicts Google on token expiry, and the class of the narrow `calendar.app.created` scope was not confirmed. The universal write path needs no credentials at all: the app publishes a long, random, revocable ICS feed of task blocks that each user subscribes to once. iCloud write access needs an app-specific password that is revoked whenever the Apple Account password changes ([Apple](https://support.apple.com/en-us/102654)) and grants more than calendar access, so it is opt-in with a clear reconnect prompt. School Outlook stays read-only by ICS.

Email intake uses Cloudflare Email Routing into a Worker, which is available on the free plan ([Cloudflare](https://developers.cloudflare.com/email-routing/email-workers/)). A forwarding chain such as Outlook to Gmail to the app means the pipeline must surface Gmail's forwarding confirmation during setup, a detail recalled and not verified, and that sender identity cannot be authenticated after double forwarding; the app authenticates the recipient token instead.

### What can truthfully be promised

A server-side agent must read user content, so envelope encryption protects against database leaks and stolen backups but not against the operator or the model provider. Anthropic states it does not train on commercial API traffic by default and retains it for 30 days ([Anthropic data usage](https://code.claude.com/docs/en/data-usage)); for its Mythos-class models, 30-day retention applies even to zero-retention organisations, and staff access is limited to a logged path for flagged content ([Anthropic support](https://support.claude.com/articles/15425996-data-retention-practices-for-mythos-class-models)). The promise to relatives is therefore these four sentences and nothing stronger:

> "Your data is encrypted in the database with a key that is unique to you."
>
> "The operator has no page that shows your content, and any time a human or the agent reads your data it is written to a log you can see. The operator technically could get at it, because the agent has to."
>
> "What the agent reads is sent to Anthropic to be processed. Anthropic keeps it up to 30 days, does not use it to train models, and its staff do not read it unless automated safety systems flag it."
>
> "Your diary, if you turn on the lock, can only be opened with your passphrase. Not the operator, not the agent, not Anthropic."

Do not promise that nobody can ever read non-diary data or that Anthropic stores nothing. Forwarded mail contains other people's data and falls under the same retention. The locked diary costs real function: no diary-based patterns in the weekly review, no server-side search, and total loss if both passphrase and recovery code are lost. Mood data is a candidate for the same opt-in tier. The audit log lives in the app's own database as an append-only table inside each user's data, shown as a plain "what your assistant did" timeline with undo, because vendor log retention of 1 to 30 days is too short; operator telemetry carries no content. Poke, the leading consumer product, reportedly has no audit log at all ([Layer3 Labs](https://www.layer3labs.io/guides/poke-ai-explained), a secondary guide). No third-party skills run in this deployment, prompts go to no outside tracing service, and a one-tap "pause the agent" stops everything except queuing.

## Technical spikes to run before committing

- **Linked billing eligibility:** whether a small, unincorporated private app can register during the beta, what scopes and token lifetimes apply, and whether prompts are visible in the owner's Console.
- **Multi-tenant Agent SDK with per-session keys:** whether the bundled Claude Code binary is practical on a shared server with a different key per session, measured against the hand-written Messages API loop.
- **Offline writes in the sync layer:** PowerSync client support for the web app and for Swift; the actual offline behaviour of Zero and Convex, which was recalled and not checked.
- **Encryption against sync rules:** how application-layer encryption coexists with sync filters that need clear columns, and where decryption keys live on the client.
- **Google Calendar scopes:** the sensitivity class of the events scope and of `calendar.app.created` in the Cloud console, and token lifetime for an unverified app in production status.
- **iOS web push:** delivery reliability, whether action buttons work, and the install and permission flow when a link opens in an in-app browser.
- **Telegram on iPhone:** private-chat topics, widgets and mini apps in practice; confirmation that a bot cannot message before the user presses Start and that bot chats are ordinary cloud chats.
- **Apple Shortcuts:** share-sheet input types, import questions for the per-user token, Health sample reads and time-based automations on iOS 26.
- **Email chain:** Gmail's forwarding confirmation, the school's actual external-forwarding setting, and what survives double forwarding.
- **Calendar plumbing:** Apple Calendar's refresh interval for subscribed ICS feeds, and server-side iCloud CalDAV with an app-specific password.
- **Extraction quality and injection:** confidence thresholds tuned on the group's own data, and a red-team pass against the quarantined reader.
- **Auth:** passkey support in the chosen auth provider, with the magic-link fallback.
- **Native route, if chosen:** automated TestFlight re-upload, beta review with a demo account, and the consent flow for health-derived data under guidelines 5.1.2(i) and 5.1.3.
- **Relay terms, if chosen:** Sendblue's and Linq's written terms on outbound-initiated messages.
- **Model prices:** the current price table, confirmed before building the spend meter.

## Conclusion

The research reframes the project. The original list of modules reads as a feature plan, but the evidence says the binding constraint is the owner's daily input budget, and the scope itself is where overcommitment is most likely to reappear. The strongest design moves are subtractions: plan only three things in detail, batch messages into three slots, turn on two tracking modules at first, require debriefs rarely, and let the system pause itself. The agent's distinctive value follows from the same logic. It is not that it plans more, but that it does the aversive first step, argues from recorded numbers and not opinion, and keeps delivering on days when the user gives nothing.

The platform picture is less settled than the behavioural one. Linked billing is a day old, Apple approved its first AI agent on Messages for Business only in June 2026, and several facts the stack depends on were recalled and not checked. The abstractions that make those shifts cheap are a channel-agnostic message queue, a provider-agnostic model boundary and a server that stays the source of truth, and they are worth more than any single vendor choice made now.
